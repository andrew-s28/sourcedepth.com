I was struck by a monologue while I was watching episode six of the final season of *Hacks*, during which comedy writer Ava Daniels goes off on a venture capitalist pitching his new AI app QuickScribbl:

> See, that is a big part of why I hate it [AI]. This forced inevitability. People like you are always saying that it's happening whether you like it or not, but you're the ones making it happen.

There was much more said on the topic in the episode, and Hannah Einbinder (the real-life counterpart of Ava Daniels) [has plenty more to say on AI as well](https://www.slashfilm.com/2138871/hacks-hannah-einbinder-harsh-words-ai/). However, this idea of forced inevitability goes well beyond just the corporate push for AI &mdash; it's a classic playbook for how corporations, and the people running them, have pushed all kinds of unhealthy technologies in front of consumers while shirking any responsibility for their role in creating the status quo in the first place.

Today, America's love of cars is looked on by many as a kind of natural selection, an inevitable move towards the optimal mode of transportation. But it wasn't always this way. In the early 20th century, city streets were bustling hubs of people and community. Our legal system backed up this cityscape, with judges doling out harsh penalties for drivers who harmed pedestrians. Eventually, as car-related injuries and deaths continued to climb, Cincinnati residents pushed for a law requiring speed governors on cars.

Automakers and dealerships, amid declining sales and sensing public sentiment turning against them, pushed hard for voters to strike down the speed governor law, eventually succeeding. This success was in no small part due to the PR campaign started by the then-budding auto lobby.

One such advertisement from the time, in addition to spreading xenophobia, chose to argue that proponents of the initiative were "against progress". Just like their modern corporate counterparts, automakers of the time had a clear interest in selling the future as one tied to the automobile, [eventually succeeding with that goal](https://thewaroncars.org/episode-161-the-creation-of-americas-car-culture-final-web-transcript/).

Today, AI companies and their proponents continue to use lessons learned from these PR campaigns, choosing to frame opposition to their products as against progress and inevitable. Their commercials feature themes of space travel and cancer research, of bold new directions and life-changing inventions.

What their commercials usually don't feature are the [artists whose work has been stolen](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2025/03/libgen-meta-openai/682093/) or the [college students who are offloading critical thinking skills](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2025/11/is-ai-dulling-our-minds/) or the myriad other as-yet-unrealized societal costs of widespread AI use. Just like the car companies of the early 1900s, these companies want their product associated with progress, advancement, *the future*, which necessarily requires sweeping their issues under the rug.

The future that AI proponents are selling is corporate propaganda, the same as it ever was. These companies and their supporters mimic previous PR campaigns simply because they work. *Propaganda works*.

That is, unless we are concious of it. This takes effort on our part, a constant reminder to ourselves and our communities that none of this is inevtiable, that we can choose the technologies that enhance our lives and leave behind the ones that don't. That we can use AI where it makes sense to us without allowing it to reshape our society. That we can regulate it.

Today, many cities are realizing the vision of the future [they were sold by the car industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futurama_(New_York_World%27s_Fair)) in the mid-20th century was fool's gold. Undoing nearly a century of car dependent development will likely take us the better part of the next century. However, we have the chance to avoid the same mistakes today. Choose for yourself how AI makes sense to be used in your life &mdash; just don't let corporate propaganda make that choice for you.
